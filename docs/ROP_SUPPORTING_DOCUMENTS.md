## 1. ROP: Information for Parents and FAQs (S100)

Purpose: to make parents of preterm neonates aware of ROP and educate them on how they can help prevent visual loss. Healthcare workers can use these FAQs for parent education; the content can be supplemented, translated into local languages and adapted.

### 1.1 What is ROP?
- ROP is a condition of the **retina** of the eye that affects preterm and low-birth-weight babies, who are usually sick.
- The retina is a layer of special nerve cells inside the eye on which images are focused (like the screen of a cinema theatre).
- Retinal blood vessels start developing in the womb. The last three months of pregnancy are important for eye development. Vessel development completes just before normal delivery at 40 weeks.
- In a premature baby, vessels have not yet formed in part of the retina (Fig. 1).
- If the immature retina is exposed to excessive oxygen after birth, abnormal new vessels may develop (Fig. 2).
- These abnormal vessels can pull and separate the retina from the underlying layers (retinal detachment) and lead to blindness (Fig. 3).
- ROP progresses through several stages. If diagnosed and treated in time, loss of vision can be prevented in almost all cases.

### 1.2 Do all preterm babies develop ROP? Who is at risk?
- No, not all preterm babies develop ROP.
- Higher risk: babies who are sick, need oxygen or ventilator support, have infection, or do not grow and gain weight normally. Risk increases with decreasing duration of pregnancy.
- **Babies who should be screened:**
  1. Preterm babies born at or less than **34 weeks** of pregnancy
  2. If exact duration of pregnancy is not known, babies weighing less than **2000 grams** at birth
  3. Babies born at **35 or 36 weeks** who were sick and required **prolonged oxygen**

### 1.3 How will I know if my child has ROP?
- Neither parents nor staff can tell, because the eyes look normal; the problem is inside the eye.
- It must be detected through a special test (ROP screening) by an eye doctor.
- **The first screening examination must take place by day 28 of life.**
- It can be done in the NICU / SCNU, even if the baby is inside the incubator. If the baby was discharged earlier, it is done at follow-up on an out-patient basis. An appointment is given at discharge.

### 1.4 How is the screening test done?
- Done by a trained eye specialist using an **Indirect Ophthalmoscope** (lens and light system).
- About **one hour before** the examination, eye drops are put in the eyes to dilate the pupils.

### 1.5 Will the test cause pain?
- No needle or injection, no blood taken.
- The baby may have mild discomfort and may cry because of handling.
- For comfort: test done about **1 hour after a feed**; baby swaddled and supported by an assistant; baby may be allowed to suck on a soother; local eye pain-relieving drops may be given.
- Procedure lasts **5-10 minutes**. Baby is closely observed for about **15 minutes** afterwards.
- Most babies tolerate it very well. A small number, especially very low-weight babies on respiratory support, can vomit or have short apnea. This is self-limiting and babies recover within **12 to 24 hours**.

### 1.6 Can any eye doctor examine for ROP?
- No. Screening requires special training in using the indirect ophthalmoscope and expertise in handling small babies. Most trained retina specialists can do it.

### 1.7 Is one examination enough?
- A single examination may not be sufficient; most babies need repeat examinations.
- First-exam findings guide the eye doctor on subsequent examinations.
- Usually re-examined **every 1 to 3 weeks** until retinal vessels have developed completely and normally.
- The number of examinations depends on how premature the baby is and on previous findings. A child who develops ROP needs more follow-up visits.
- The eye doctor gives the appointment for the next examination.

### 1.8 What happens if ROP is diagnosed?
- The eye specialist assesses severity.
- The majority of babies have mild ROP that gets better without treatment.
- A very small number develop severe ROP needing **LASER** to prevent blindness.
- If ROP is severe enough to threaten vision, **LASER should be done within 48-72 hours of diagnosis**, depending on severity. LASER is not effective later if diagnosis is delayed and the child presents at a late stage.
- In advanced stages where LASER may not be effective, the eye doctor will explain other options, including surgery to save vision.

### 1.9 What is LASER treatment?
- A high-energy light beam, used for about 30 years to treat eye conditions; described as very precise and safe.
- Done on a **day-care basis** by trained eye specialists in select centres. Baby is closely monitored.
- Like screening, the doctor views the retina through a special lens and focuses LASER energy through the same lens onto the retina.
- Takes **1-2 hours** depending on severity. Baby can go home the same day or by the next day at the latest.
- LASER stops abnormal retinal vessels from growing further, preventing complications such as retinal separation. The abnormal vessels disappear, greatly reducing the risk of vision loss.

### 1.10 Outcome and long-term follow-up
- LASER is quite safe and the standard of care for several eye disorders including ROP; used for ROP worldwide for two decades. Majority of babies have good vision afterwards.
- Untreated advanced ROP can cause complete visual loss.
- Preterm babies are also at higher risk of other eye conditions: **squint** and **refractive errors** (spectacles). This also applies to those treated with LASER; these conditions can be treated.
- Premature infants, especially those who had ROP, should have an **eye check-up every 6 months**. Long-term follow-up may be needed.

### 1.11 What do doctors and nurses do to reduce ROP risk?
- Regulate oxygen using **pulse oximetry** (oxygen saturation monitoring).
- Take special precautions to prevent infection.
- Promote mother's milk feeding.
- Monitor growth closely.
- Advise **Kangaroo care** (skin-to-skin contact).

### 1.12 What can parents do?
Prematurity is an inherent risk, so ROP is not entirely preventable. Parents can reduce risk by:
- **Preventing infection:** own hygiene and thorough hand-washing before touching the baby.
- **Giving mother's own milk:** reduces infection and provides nutrition for growth.
- **Kangaroo care** (skin-to-skin with mother, father or any other adult): promotes growth and regular breathing, associated with reduced chances of ROP.
- **Following ROP screening appointments:** timely screening and treatment is crucial. ROP can progress rapidly and has no external symptoms or signs until late; the baby may look fine yet have ROP. Delay may cause incurable blindness in both eyes, for which no treatment is available at present. Eye transplantation is not possible for ROP blindness.
- If a preterm baby was **not advised a ROP check-up by 3 to 4 weeks of age**, ask whether the baby needs one.

## 2. ICROP3 Classification

Each eye should be classified by **zone, plus disease, stage and extent**. If aggressive ROP (A-ROP) is present, it should be noted.

### 2.1 Zone
- **a.** 3 retinal zones centred on the optic disc. The location of the **most posterior** retinal vascularization or ROP lesion denotes the zone for the eye.
- **b.** **Posterior zone II**: begins at the margin between zone I and zone II and extends into zone II for **2 disc diameters**.
- **c.** **"Notch"**: an incursion by the ROP lesion of **1-2 clock hours** into a more posterior zone. The zone is noted by the most posterior zone of retinal vascularization with the qualifier "notch" (e.g. "zone I secondary to notch").

### 2.2 Plus and Pre-plus disease
- **Plus disease:** dilation and tortuosity of retinal vessels.
- **Pre-plus disease:** abnormal vascular dilation and/or tortuosity insufficient for plus disease.
- Retinal vascular changes represent a **continuous spectrum** from normal to pre-plus to plus, with sample images showing the range.
- Assess using vessels **within zone I**, not only vessels within the field of narrow-angle photographs, and not by number of quadrants of abnormality.

### 2.3 Stage of acute disease (stages 1-3)
Defined by the structure at the vascular-avascular juncture:

| Stage | Appearance |
|-------|-----------|
| 1 | Demarcation line |
| 2 | Ridge |
| 3 | Extraretinal neovascular proliferation or flat neovascularization |

If more than one stage is present in the same eye, the eye is classified by the **most severe** stage.

### 2.4 Aggressive ROP (A-ROP)
- Previously called aggressive-posterior ROP (AP-ROP): a severe, rapidly progressive form located in posterior zone I or zone II.
- Because it may occur beyond the posterior retina and in larger preterm infants (particularly in regions with limited resources), the Committee recommends the new term **A-ROP**.

### 2.5 Retinal detachment (stages 4-5)
- **Stage 4** (partial): **4A** fovea attached; **4B** fovea detached.
- **Stage 5** (total):
  - **5A:** optic disc visible by ophthalmoscopy (suggests open-funnel detachment)
  - **5B:** optic disc not visible due to retrolental fibrovascular tissue or closed-funnel detachment
  - **5C:** 5B with anterior segment changes (e.g. marked anterior chamber shallowing, irido-corneo-lenticular adhesions, corneal opacification), suggesting closed-funnel configuration

### 2.6 Extent of disease
- Defined as **12 sectors** using clock-hour designations.

### 2.7 Regression
- Definition of ROP regression and its sequelae, whether spontaneous or after laser or anti-VEGF treatment.
- Regression can be complete or incomplete.
- Location and extent of **persistent avascular retina (PAR)** should be documented.

### 2.8 Reactivation
- Nomenclature for ROP reactivation after treatment, which may include new ROP lesions and vascular changes.
- When reactivation of ROP stages occurs, the modifier **"reactivated"** is recommended (e.g. "reactivated stage 2").

### 2.9 Long-term sequelae
- Emphasised beyond previous ICROP versions: late retinal detachments, PAR, macular anomalies, retinal vascular changes and glaucoma.

### 2.10 Schema (figure)
Schema of right eye (RE) and left eye (LE) showing zone borders and clock-hour sectors used to describe the location of vascularization and extent of retinopathy.
- Labels: Zone I, Zone II, Zone III, Posterior Zone II, Macula, Optic Disc, Ora Serrata, clock hours 12 / 3 / 6 / 9.
- Solid circles = borders of zones I-III; dotted circles = borders of posterior zone II (two disc diameters beyond zone I).
- Example shown in the left eye: approximately **three clock hours of stage 1 disease in zone II** (a single line on the drawing documents stage 1).

## 4. Notes for the app (observations, not rules)

These are observations from comparing the documents. Confirm with your supervisor before using any of them in the app.

1. **First-screening timing:** the parent FAQ says first screening "by day 28 of life". The ROP STW PDF (as summarised earlier in this project) gives 4 weeks postnatal age, and 2-3 weeks if GA <28 weeks or BW <1200 g. The FAQ does not give the earlier window. Keep the STW values.
2. **Screening criteria:** the FAQ matches the STW on GA ≤34 weeks and BW <2000 g, and mentions 35-36 weeks with "prolonged oxygen". The STW wording for 34-36 weeks includes risk factors (oxygen, sepsis, transfusion etc.). Keep the STW wording.
3. **Treatment window:** the FAQ's "LASER within 48-72 hours" matches the STW's 48-72 hours.
4. **Long-term follow-up:** the "eye check-up every 6 months" advice appears only in the FAQ, not in the STW summary. Show it only as parent-education text, clearly labelled with its source.
5. **ICROP3 vs form:** the screening form uses "Zone 1-3", "Stage 1-5", Pre-plus, Plus, Total clock hours and APROP, which map onto ICROP3 terms. The form has no fields for notch, posterior zone II, stage 4A/4B/5A-5C, regression, reactivation or PAR; those are in ICROP3 only.
6. **Possible uses (only if the supervisor agrees):**
   - A read-only "Parent information" screen from Section 1, with its source cited.
   - A read-only ICROP3 glossary from Section 2.
   - A data-entry form based on Section 3. If built, it must store real data in the database (no mock data) and be validated; it is not part of the STW decision logic.
